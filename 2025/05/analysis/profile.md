# Conclusions

## Grain - establish this before any metric

**`Fact_Sales` is 366 rows: one row per calendar day of 2024.** 2024 was a leap year; `Transaction_Date`
is the only unique-and-complete column and covers 2024-01-01 → 2024-12-31 with no gaps. That is the
true primary key.

This is the month's most consequential fact, and the published field got it wrong. Several entries
report "366 transactions" as a business metric - it is just the row count. Others report
"303 customers" - there is **no customer identifier anywhere in the data**; 303 is the distinct count
of `Transaction_ID`, which is not unique (see DQ2). Every per-customer statistic in the benchmark
field is unfounded.

Each row bundles one day's activity into a single (model, colour, storage, channel, payment, one
customer's age/gender, one city) tuple. So a "customer profile by brand" (R3, R7, R9) rests on ~73
rows per brand, each describing a single notional customer.

## Top 5 data quality issues shaping this analysis

**DQ1 · The grain is daily, not transactional - 366 rows, not 18,548.**
`Units_Sold` (1-99, mean 50.7) is a quantity *attribute* of the day's row, not a row count. Total
units 18,548 and revenue $14,525,413 are correct sums, but every rate, share, and cross-tab has an
effective n of **366**, not 18,548. Sizing significance off 18K is the field's core error.

**DQ2 · `Transaction_ID` is not unique, despite the data dictionary calling it "Unique transaction ID".**
303 distinct values across 366 rows; 54 IDs are reused, one appearing 4×. It cannot be a key and
cannot be counted as anything. It is the origin of the field's phantom "303 customers".

**DQ3 · `Units_Sold` looks like Uniform(1, 99) pooled, and separates only one brand.**
KS test of the pooled column against uniform: **p = 0.45** (mean 50.68 vs 50.0 expected, sample
variance 733.5 vs 800 expected) - a generator artifact, not demand. That test is on the *marginal*
and never sees the brand label, so the between-brand question needs its own tests:
Kruskal-Wallis on units/day by brand gives **H = 11.789, p = 0.0190**, and a 20,000-draw permutation
null on unit share (seed 0) clears exactly one brand - **OnePlus 23.70% against a null of
[18.00, 22.39], p = 0.002** (Bonferroni 0.009). Apple 22.78%, Xiaomi 19.03%, Samsung 17.50% and
Google 16.99% all sit inside theirs. Day-row allocation is even (83/74/71/70/68, χ² = 1.896,
p = 0.755), so OnePlus's lead is not a coverage artifact. **"OnePlus is the top-selling brand" is
supported; every ranking below first place is not.**

> *Corrected 2025-05-27.* This paragraph previously read "any claim of the form 'X sells more units
> than Y' is noise", justified by overlapping binomial 95% CIs (±4.36pp etc.). A unit share here is a
> ratio of sums over 366 day-rows, not a proportion of 366 Bernoulli trials, so a binomial CI is the
> wrong interval - it was too wide and separated nobody. See `analysis/insights.md` I-1.

**DQ4 · Country coverage is too thin below the top two to report.**
India 169 rows · Turkey 136 · **Bangladesh 51 · Pakistan 10**. Pakistan's 10 rows are spread across
5 cities, so Multan and Rawalpindi are **n = 1**. City-level comparison is only defensible for India
and Turkey; anything ranked below that is a single-observation artifact. The field ranked all 25
cities regardless.

**DQ5 · Both dimension tables are fully redundant.**
`Dim_Locations` (25 rows) and `Dim_Products` (274 rows) contain no column absent from `Fact_Sales`,
and referential integrity is clean in both directions (0 orphans). `Dim_Locations` uses all 25 cities,
so it adds precisely nothing. `Dim_Products` contains 82 variants with zero sales - **but that is not
an assortment finding**: drawing 366 rows uniformly from 274 variants predicts 71.9 unsold, and 82 is
well inside sampling noise. The "star schema" is decorative; we will model it deliberately at G4
rather than inherit it.

## Also true, and useful

- **`Operating_System` is 100% determined by `Brand`.** Apple → iOS (83 rows), all four others →
  Android (283). Requirement R2's "Android vs iOS" is a disguised "Apple vs everyone else". Any
  OS chart is a brand chart with fewer categories.
- **The price architecture is real, and it is the only strong signal in the file.** Price is
  model-determined with ~3% jitter (median within-model CV = 0.032), spanning Redmi Note 13 $327 →
  Z Fold 6 $1,844. Brand price separation is overwhelming (ANOVA **p ≈ 1.3e-24**): Samsung $1,090
  mean, Apple $876, Google $771, OnePlus $681, Xiaomi $490.
- **Rate vs volume genuinely diverge, and this survives the noise** because it is driven by the price
  ladder rather than by random units: OnePlus leads units (4,395) but **Apple leads revenue**
  ($3.64M); Xiaomi is 3rd in units and **last** in revenue ($1.82M). Samsung is 4th in units and 2nd
  in revenue on a barbell range (Galaxy M15 $346 ↔ Z Fold 6 $1,844).
- **No seasonality.** Monthly revenue CV = 0.11, min/max = 0.70, December ($1.30M) ≈ January ($1.41M).
  For a retailer spanning India and Turkey, the *absence* of a Diwali or year-end peak is itself the
  finding - and a warning that R8's month-over-month analysis is chasing noise.
- **Of 11 cross-tabs tested, 2 survive Bonferroni correction** (α = 0.05/11 = 0.004545):
  - `Sales_Channel` × `Customer_Age_Group` - χ²=28.79, **p=0.0013**. Retail Store over-indexes on
    18-25 (24.0% of its rows vs 16.4% online); Partner over-indexes on 34-41 (26.4%).
  - `Brand` × `Customer_Gender` - χ²=22.22, **p=0.004517**, which clears α by a hair and should be
    called marginal, not established. The pattern is a male skew for the two brands that lead on
    units (OnePlus 54.1% male, Apple 49.4%) against a female skew for the rest (Xiaomi 60.0% female,
    Google 58.8%, Samsung 56.3%).

  Both are fragile: Partner is 53 rows in total, min expected cell 5.5, and `Other` gender is 32 rows
  spread over 5 brands. Treat as leads to test at G3, not conclusions. Everything else - including
  `Brand`×`Country`, `Storage_Size`×`Brand`, and `Payment_Type`×`Age_Group` - is independent.
- `Customer_Age` → `Customer_Age_Group` mapping is perfectly consistent; the bucket labelled
  "58-65" tops out at 64. `Total_Revenue` = `Price` × `Units_Sold` exactly, 0 mismatches.

## What this means for G3

The honest position is that this dataset supports far fewer claims than the field made of it. The
work at G3 is to separate the structural facts (price ladder, mix, OS≡Brand, coverage limits) from
the random draws (unit volumes, brand rankings, monthly trend, most cross-tabs) - and to build a
report whose thesis rests only on the former, while making the uncertainty visible rather than
hiding it. That is the differentiator, not a limitation.
