## Top data quality issues shaping this analysis

Every number below is reproduced by `analysis/integrity.py` (all checks pass).

### DQ1 - The file is two datasets stapled together

Columns 1-13 behave like a real lending book. Columns 14-29 behave like a random number
generator. The split was **declared before testing** (`integrity.py`, `ORIGINAL` / `APPENDED`).

| Block | Columns tested | Max effect on `loan_status` | **Median effect** | Clear Bonferroni (α=2.0e-3) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **Original** | 11 | 0.415 (`loan_grade`) | **0.144** | **10 / 11** |
| **Appended** | 14 | 0.386 | **0.009** | **3 / 14** |

A 16× difference in median effect. Three independent tells line up with the same boundary:

1. **Nulls.** 3,116 in `loan_int_rate` and 895 in `person_emp_length` - and **zero** across all
   fourteen appended columns. Real data has gaps; generated data does not.
2. **Impossible values.** `person_age` reaches **144** and `person_emp_length` reaches **123**
   (7 rows, 0.02%) - the characteristic messiness of a real-world extract. Every appended column
   sits inside clean declared bounds: `credit_utilization_ratio` ∈ [0.05, 0.95],
   `past_delinquencies` ∈ [0, 6], `loan_term_months` ∈ {12, 24, 36, 60}.
3. **The publisher says so.** The Data Dictionary calls `other_debt` *"Simulated"*.

The original block also matches the row count (32,581), default rate (21.82%) and null pattern of
a widely-circulated public credit-risk dataset. **That coincidence is not load-bearing** - every
claim below rests on measured column behaviour, not on provenance (`assumptions.md` A-3).

### DQ2 - Both ratios the brief asks about are arithmetic on columns already present

Brief **Q3** asks how loan-to-income *and* debt-to-income relate to repayment, as though they
were two questions.

| Column | Identity | Max error |
|---|---|---:|
| `loan_to_income_ratio` | `loan_amnt` / `person_income` | **9.7e-17** |
| `debt_to_income_ratio` | (`other_debt` + `loan_amnt`) / `person_income` | **4.4e-16** |
| `other_debt` | `person_income` × U(0.05, 0.30) | - |

`loan_to_income_ratio` correlates **0.9989** with the original `loan_percent_income` - the same
column at more decimal places. And because `debt_to_income_ratio` is that quantity plus a uniform
draw, it predicts **worse** than the column it dilutes: **0.3215 vs 0.3859**. Adding simulated
debt destroys information. Q3 is one question, and the answer is loan-to-income.

### DQ3 - Two columns claim to measure credit history; one is a random draw

Brief **Q5** asks how past defaults affect loan outcomes. The file offers two candidates.

| `past_delinquencies` | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| n | 19,702 | 9,829 | 2,586 | 405 | 54 | 4 | 1 |
| default % | 21.77 | 22.02 | 21.19 | 23.21 | 22.22 | 25.0 | 0.0 |

Two different tests, each labelled with what produced it: the 7×2 contingency table against
`loan_status` gives χ² **p = 0.9513**, Cramér's V = **0.0070**; the |point-biserial| used for the
cross-column comparison in DQ1 gives **r = 0.0005, p = 0.933** - rank 25 of 25. It also fits
Poisson(λ=0.505141) with goodness-of-fit **p = 0.5081** (`integrity.py` check 9a).

Meanwhile the original credit-history column *looks* like it works as a lender would expect:
`cb_person_default_on_file` = N → **18.39%**, Y → **37.81%** - a **2.06×** crude lift, V = 0.179,
p = 3.9e-229.

> **That 2.06× is composition, not signal, and it must never be quoted without this line.**
> Grades A and B contain **0 of 21,228** prior defaulters. Stratify by grade and the lift
> disappears: within C-F the two levels differ by at most **3.55pp** (grade E), and in four of
> those five grades the prior defaulters default *less*. `cb_person_default_on_file` is an input
> to the grade, so conditioning on grade removes it. See `integrity.py` check 9(b),
> `test_REJECTED_prior_default_is_grade_composition`, and `insights.md` I13.

An analyst answering Q5 with the more precise-sounding column concludes that prior delinquency
does not predict default; an analyst answering it with the crude lift concludes that it doubles
your risk. Both are wrong, in opposite directions. **The file's answer to Q5 is that it has
none** - three candidate columns, one a Poisson draw, one `person_age` at r=0.859 under another
name, one the credit grade viewed sideways.

### DQ4 - The nulls are a risk signal, and everyone will drop them

| Column | Missing | Default rate, missing | Default rate, present | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `person_emp_length` | 895 | **31.51%** [28.47, 34.66] | 21.54% | **+9.97pp**, p=1.5e-12 |
| `loan_int_rate` | 3,116 | 20.67% [19.26, 22.13] | 21.94% | -1.27pp, p=0.11 (n.s.) |

Missing employment length carries a **1.46× default lift** and outranks every appended column in
this file except the two derived ratios. The standard first move - drop incomplete rows - throws
away the riskiest 895 applicants in the book. **No rows are dropped here** (`assumptions.md` A-5).

### DQ5 - There are no rejected applications

The file is booked loans only. It contains no applicant who was declined, so it cannot speak to
approval rates, to who was turned away, or on what basis. This bounds every question the brief
asks about lending *policy* - and it bounds the fairness question hardest of all, because a
disparate-impact review is largely a question about who never made it into the book.

## Conclusions - what this file can and cannot support

**Cannot support:** approval-stage lending fairness (DQ5 - there are no rejected applicants,
which holds regardless of the fabrication argument); any finding from `past_delinquencies`,
which fits Poisson(λ=0.505141) with goodness-of-fit p=0.5081 (DQ3); **any claim that
`cb_person_default_on_file` carries risk information independent of the grade** - the 2.06×
lift is grade composition (DQ3); any treatment of debt-to-income as distinct from
loan-to-income (DQ2); any geographic, employment-type, education or marital-status risk finding.

**Can support**, all from the original block:

1. **The grade ladder has a cliff, not a slope.** A 9.96% → G 98.44%, but the step from **C to D
   is a 2.85× jump in default** (20.73% → 59.05%) for **+1.90pp** of interest rate. Grades D-G
   are 15.0% of the book (4,895 loans) and default 49-88pp above grade A while priced only
   8-13pp above it. *Caveat carried everywhere this appears:* the raw D rate of 59.05% is
   partly the two boolean rules - remove them and D is **46.33%** (`insights.md` I9). The cliff
   survives; the raw figure must not be published as a risk gradient.
2. **Loan-to-income is the strongest continuous predictor** (0.386) and is not improved by any
   appended variant.
3. **Home ownership matters** (V=0.242) - and it matters as an *interaction* with
   loan-to-income, not as a main effect: renters step from 28.5% to 100.0% between an LTI of
   0.30 and 0.31 while mortgage-holders cross the same line at 15.7% → 21.6% (`insights.md` I5).
   **`cb_person_default_on_file` is NOT on this list.** Its V=0.179 is real and its 2.06× lift
   is grade composition - see the box in DQ3. It belongs above, under *cannot support*.
4. **Missing employment length is itself a risk signal** (DQ4).
5. **Two boolean rules decide 42.0371% of every default** - the month's thesis, and the reason
   1 and 3 have to be stated with their de-contaminated figures. `insights.md` I1-I3.
