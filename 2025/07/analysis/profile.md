# Conclusions

`analysis/integrity.py`, which reproduces every figure quoted.*

## Grain

**One row per customer: 120 customers, 12 columns.** `Customer_ID` is genuinely unique (120/120) -
the first month where the ID column survives the standing check, after May's `Transaction_ID` and
June's `Post_ID` both failed it. No dates, despite the brief describing "feedback throughout 2024",
so there is no time dimension at all.

## The five issues that shape this analysis

**DQ1 · n = 120. That is the finding.**
The brief asks nine questions that each compare groups. After splitting by any single factor the
groups are 10-72 customers; by two factors, 19 of 30 `Location` × `Loyalty_Level` cells hold fewer
than five customers and one is empty. **This dataset is roughly a third of May's and a fiftieth of
June's.**

**DQ2 · `Satisfaction_Score` is statistically indistinguishable from a uniform draw over 1-10.**
χ² against uniform = 7.00 on 9 df, **p = 0.637**. Mean 5.35 against the 5.50 a uniform expects, sd
3.03 against 2.87. The scores are not concentrated anywhere; there is no modal "satisfied" cluster,
no skew, nothing. This is the same species of artifact as May's `Units_Sold`.

**DQ3 · Not one of seven candidate drivers survives multiple-comparison correction.**

| Axis | Kruskal p | η² | vs Bonferroni α=0.00625 |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `Satisfaction_Factor` | 0.0231 | 0.1540 | does **not** survive |
| `Loyalty_Level` | 0.0863 | 0.0399 | does not survive |
| `Purchase_History` | 0.2892 | 0.0111 | does not survive |
| `Group` | 0.4887 | 0.0037 | does not survive |
| `Gender` | 0.5226 | 0.0032 | does not survive |
| `Location` | 0.7219 | 0.0514 | does not survive |
| `Support_Contacted` | **0.9557** | **0.0000** | does not survive |

`Age` correlates with satisfaction at **r = +0.020** (p = 0.83).

**DQ4 · `Loyalty_Level` is non-monotonic against satisfaction.**
High 5.65 · **Low 5.84** · Medium 4.47. If loyalty bore any relationship to satisfaction the order
would be monotonic. Low-loyalty customers report the *highest* mean satisfaction. That is what noise
looks like, and it is the direct answer to requirement **R8**.

**DQ5 · The brief's headline question has an effect size of essentially zero.**
R4 asks whether contacting support hurts satisfaction. Contacted 5.36 (n=56) vs not 5.34 (n=64) -
a difference of **0.013 points on a 10-point scale**, Cohen's d = 0.004, p = 0.96. Not "small";
indistinguishable from zero.

## The useful part: the study cannot answer its own questions

This is where a null result becomes actionable. At 80% power and α=0.05, with ~60 customers per
group:

| To detect a difference of | You would need per group | You have |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.5 points | 575 | ~60 |
| 1.0 points | 144 | ~60 |
| 1.5 points | 64 | ~60 |
| 2.0 points | 36 | ~60 |

**The smallest difference this survey could reliably detect is 1.55 points** - around a sixth of the
entire scale. A support experience that genuinely cost a full point of satisfaction would be
invisible here. So the correct deliverable is not a satisfaction dashboard; it is a measurement
design.

## The one axis that flirts with significance, and why it is not kept

`Satisfaction_Factor` reaches p=0.023 with η²=0.154 - Product Quality 7.55 down to Ease of Use 4.07.
It is rejected on three grounds: it does not survive correction across the seven axes tested
(α=0.00625 - see insights.md I-2 for why the family is eight, not seven); it splits 120 customers
into **ten** groups averaging 12 each, the smallest being 10;
and a permutation test putting random labels on the same data returns η² ≥ 0.154 about **2.5%** of
the time against a median null of 0.070 - so the effect is barely distinguishable from what
relabelling at random produces. It is reported in the UI with that caveat attached, not as a driver.
